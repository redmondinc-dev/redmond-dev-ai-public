Who owns what

Agreed 10 September — responsibilities sit where the lane already is
Data Team
All data — databases, data sources, APIs including Paylocity, permissions, performance, and monitoring access. Need a database or a data source? Go here.
IT Team
Security, roles, permissions, licences, and application servers. Need access or a role? Go here.
App Team
Third-party applications such as Acumatica.
Development Team
Code, and the registry — routing a broken app to whoever can fix it, and running the annual review.
You, the builder
Your own app through Lanes 1 and 2, including first-line support for the app itself.
The data team owns the process, not the data. A request comes to them, they check with the actual owner, and then grant — one front door instead of four hundred.

What is happening right now

Where we are

The registry is seeded. We now know, roughly, what exists across Redmond — apps, APIs, MCP servers, databases — and the work has shifted from cataloguing to building the things that keep it current.

Running alongside that, we are working out what the lanes mean in practice and answering the governance questions underneath: who owns each data source, and who decides access.

The audit kept growing. The register holds 263 records — 196 of them software (apps, integrations, data sources and services), the other 67 projects and musings. Of the software, 109 is Lane 2, 20 is Lane 3, and 67 has no lane assigned yet. Early estimates were ten or twenty. Lane 1 holds nothing, by design: it needs no registration.

Seeding is done. The registry is populated and the lane fields are on the entry template. The next step is moving it off Confluence — decided on 22 October, the permanent home is the existing projects site extended, not a new build.

The harness has started. Discovery closed on 22 October and the build is underway. Standards reach the builder's agent through the MCP server and organization-level skills; only two things hard-fail a merge — missing SSO and publicly exposed credentials. Everything else reports without blocking.

Security priorities are ranked, and that story is closed. Acumatica roles first, then per-user MCP server scoping, then table-level database permissions. The agreed posture: not the bare minimum and not perfection — a layer or two above the low-hanging fruit. The one piece still live — table-level permissions — moved onto the data access story, where the access question belongs.

The model got its first real test. On 29 October the first request arrived to have an AI app write back to a production database. It was recognized and brought to the group rather than quietly connected — which is the gates working. Settled: writing to production does not automatically mean Lane 3. Blast radius decides. The agent carries its user's permissions and nothing more, the write routes through the MCP server, the account gets scoped, it gets tested, and the data team helps.

Company AI accounts, from 9 November. Redmond data becomes reachable only from a company account — no personal accounts on the MCP server. Voted in favour on 29 October. The MCP server already answers "what can I access?" per user, where a week earlier any session could see every tool.

The Lane 2 developer channel exists. Every Lane 2 builder the group knows of, plus the dev team. Peer review callouts, problems, and general programming support. For now it is also the interim answer to who supports Lane 2 — the dev team plus the channel, run as an experiment.

The project has an end date. 31 May 2027, set on 30 October. It covers Phases 1A through 2B and the Phase 3 standards, and it is the point at which the standards are published and Tech Team Coordination carries the work as ongoing operations rather than as a project.

The board was cleaned up on 30 October. Three epics rewritten to purpose only, eight stories trimmed back to one goal line plus criteria, two stories closed or merged, and one new story created for Lane 2 peer review. Every open story now sits at or under six acceptance criteria. Settled decisions live on these pages and in Confluence rather than accumulating inside the stories.

Nothing changes for anyone yet. No approvals, no permissions, no new process beyond the account change above. Visibility first.

Phase 2B — Lanes Established and SupportedRun the model in practice 8 to do · 2 closed · 1 tracked elsewhere In progress

A published model changes nothing on its own. Lanes get assigned to real things, support paths get named teams behind them, and the first apps actually move between lanes so we learn where it breaks. Lane 3 already works. Lane 2 is the gap — a useful tool that people depend on, with no clear answer on who picks it up when the owner is unavailable.

Added 22 October: the harness is the delivery mechanism for all of it. Standards reach the builder's agent through the MCP server and organization-level skills and settings, with hooks enforced in Claude Code. Only missing SSO and publicly exposed credentials hard-fail a merge; code-quality findings report without blocking. Repo provisioning carries the agent documentation and hooks from the moment a repo is created, and registration attaches there rather than as a separate step. It pilots on one repo before going organization-wide.

Added 29 October: writing back to a production system does not automatically mean Lane 3. Blast radius decides. An agent acting with its user's credentials is not new permission — it is the same permission used faster, and it cannot do more damage than that person could. A Lane 2 write routes through the MCP server, runs on a scoped account, gets tested, and goes through the data team, who review write requests individually for now. Also added: company AI accounts only from 9 November, and scoped credentials for agents doing system work rather than running as a real user.

Added 8 September: the Lane 3 handoff also decides where an app lives — embedded in Teams and Dash, or standalone. That call belongs to the team that will support it, made case by case at the point of promotion. The original builder keeps a voice on user experience and can still open pull requests.

Added 10 September, corrected since: support splits by lane. Lane 1 is unsupported. In Lane 2 the builder is first line on the app itself, IT supports the environment — access, roles, licences, hosting — and the data team supports data sources and MCP access. Neither fixes broken application code. The development team supports Lane 3, with IT underneath.

Define the Lane 2 Peer Review ProcessUnassignedDEVSTD-47

As a Lane 2 builder, I need to know who reviews my app and what they look at so that peer review is a real check rather than a rubber stamp.

Split out of DEVSTD-29 on 30 October. Peer review at Lane 2 and above was settled 22 October; this story is the human half of it.

Acceptance criteria

Automated Background Security SweepsPhilDEVSTD-42

As the development team, I need automated security sweeps running across our code so that problems surface without waiting for someone to open a merge request.

Acceptance criteria

  • A separate review lens for apps that are themselves AI agents, covering prompt injection and system prompt inspection

What is still open

  • Who supports a Lane 2 app beyond its builder. The one substantial gap left in the model. IT covers the environment and the data team covers data sources and MCP access, but no group owns the application layer once the builder is unavailable. Scoped in DEVSTD-32, not yet answered — and it is Magic 8 standard #5, Support, which the charter has carried open since September.
  • What this costs. Magic 8 standard #7 still reads "Amount TBD" from July. The end date is now set; the number behind it is not.
  • Where Lane 2 apps and their data actually live. Azure, a corporate account on a lighter hosting platform, or whatever the builder already set up — plus a database host. Raised again on 29 October with a new question attached: once our data leaves Microsoft, is that acceptable? Until this lands, "use the paved road" does not name a road. Now one story, DEVSTD-45, which spans IT and the data team — so its owner is an open question too.
  • Nothing would tell us an agent had gone off the rails. Raised 29 October. The guardrails reduce the attack surface and limit blast radius, but they are passive. Logging exists and nothing is pointed at it. No story behind this yet.
  • Where a pull request is required at all. Artifacts, dashboards and scheduled tasks never land in a repository. The working signal is that code you want to keep iterating on belongs in GitHub; a one-off does not. Not drawn properly yet.
  • Writing back to production, as a repeatable rule. Settled in principle on 29 October — blast radius decides — but write requests are being reviewed individually for now, because the volume is low and the pattern is not understood well enough to automate. No story behind it.
  • Agents with their own accounts for personal work. Settled for system access: give an agent a scoped login rather than your own. Unsettled where the agent needs to be you, with your calendar and inbox.
  • Who approves a Lane 2 pull request. Raised on 22 October and left open. Automated and AI review can run, but a person approves the merge and carries the responsibility. Nobody is named. Now carried by DEVSTD-47, which is itself unassigned.
  • How connector approval works. Personal AI accounts were decided on 29 October; connector approvals were not. Requests have been approved without discussion, and at least one put Redmond data into a service we never approved. Team accounts with per-team connector sets is the proposal, not a decision.
  • Who owns the Redmond data MCP server day to day. Phil tracks it; Bryan owns the component. The development team's position is unknown. Recurring, and not captured in a story.
  • Lane 2 builder buy-in. The model has not yet been put to the people it most affects. Lane 3 buy-in is straightforward; Lane 2 is where the change actually lands.
  • Telling Lane 2 builders the process exists. Knowing what is out there has been the hard part, and people building do not reliably know the group wants to hear from them.
  • How many lanes. Settled on 1 October — three lanes and two merges, no fourth lane. The four-level risk scale used on 22 October does not map cleanly onto three lanes, so this is not fully closed. What we call the second merge is still loose.
  • Does PII move an app out of Lane 2. The published identifiers say yes; practice has treated sensitive data as normal in Lane 2. The two readings need reconciling.
  • Who owns the registry day to day. The development team owns it in principle. Who maintains entries and chases renewals week to week is not named. Raised again on 22 October: the concern is building the thing before the people who run it exist.
  • Who provisions and pays for cloud resources. Shared ad hoc between IT and dev today. Forgotten, still-billing resources are the named risk.
  • Who approves a data access exception. The default is settled — open, except sensitive HR data and Paylocity. The decision rights are not.
  • Who builds the standardized API layer. Four parts agreed in principle on 8 September, none of them assigned.
  • External and non-associate access. Spouses, plus-ones, and partners get solved app by app today. Needs a standard.
  • Premier to Azure hosting. Roughly $7,800 a month today — about $3,100 for non-Acumatica hosting and a little over $4,000 for Acumatica — plus support hours billed per ticket. Cost is close to a wash; the case is isolation, flexibility and support value, and licensing is the deciding variable. Belongs to infrastructure rather than this project, but it shapes the hosting standard.
  • Budget. The end date is set at 31 May 2027. The cost estimate is not — the charter still reads "Amount TBD", and PROJ-160 itself has yet to carry the date.
