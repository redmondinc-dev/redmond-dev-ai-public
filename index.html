LANES: Lane 1 — Sandbox Lane 1.5 — On-Ramp Lane 2 — Paved Road Lane 3 — Engineered

→THE LANES — DETERMINED BY WHAT YOUR APP NEEDS AND TOUCHES

Do I need to register what I'm building? If it touches company data, yes. Reading probably, writing definitely — at that point it is a Lane 2 app and we want it on the list. If it does not touch company data, you are in Lane 1 and there is nothing to do. Experiment away.
LANE 1 — SANDBOXExplore freely
Who builds
Anyone. No approval, no ticket, no registration.
Data allowed
  • Synthetic or public data only
  • No production data, read or write
  • No secrets, no production credentials
Where it runs
Pre-provisioned sandbox or local. Free tier.
Who owns and supports it
You own it. Nobody supports it — that is the trade for total freedom.
Nothing to register
No list, no form, no permission. Lane 1 stays invisible on purpose.
Lane 1.5 — the on-ramp
  • Static site on a free tier, handed over instantly
  • Redmond-flavoured material, never real data
  • Guided development for someone with an idea
  • Needing more generates a ticket → Lane 2
LANE 2 — PAVED ROADWhere most business-unit apps live
Who builds
Anyone building with AI tooling, inside the standards.
Data allowed
  • Internal data through approved connectors
  • Role-scoped through the MCP server
  • Never a direct database connection
What registration requires
  • Named business owner and technical steward
  • Environment separation and audit logging
  • Renewal after twelve months, or it is disabled
Where it runs
Redmond tenancy, SSO enforced, sanctioned hosting.
Who owns and supports it
You own it. IT supports it — the platform, hosting, identity, connections, and advice on the logic when you need it.
LANE 3 — ENGINEEREDBuilt and owned by the dev team
Who builds
Development team, with business experts embedded.
What lands here
  • Mission critical, customer facing, or financial
  • Time System, ROPE — a full project
  • Magic 8, sprints, Confluence and Jira
Data allowed
Any data, under full controls. Direct API rather than MCP, for efficiency.
Controls
  • Architecture review and security sign-off
  • Full SDLC, disaster recovery, monitoring
  • Service level agreement
Who owns and supports it
The development team owns and supports it — fully, with an SLA behind it.
Your lane follows what you are building, not who you are. Three things decide it: the data your app reaches, who comes to depend on it, and what happens if it stops. A developer on the dev team building a personal utility is in Lane 1. A business-unit analyst building something the company runs on is in Lane 3. Experience and job title do not come into it.

And the point of all this is more apps, not fewer. There are roughly twenty Lane 2 apps at Redmond today. If this works, there should be two hundred within a year — built by people across the business, without waiting on the development team. The lanes exist to make that safe, not to slow it down.

→MOVING BETWEEN LANES — WHAT IT TAKES

LANE 1 → LANE 2Ticket, approve, provision
1. You outgrow the sandbox
You need a real resource or a live connector — synthetic data is no longer enough.
2. The request makes a ticket
Generated for you, not filled in by you.
3. We approve and provision
  • Azure resources deployed for you
  • Role-scoped data access granted
  • Registry entry created as a by-product
4. You now have an owner and a clock
Named owner recorded, twelve-month renewal starts.
STAYING IN LANE 2What keeps it healthy
Keep it supportable
  • Documentation someone else can follow
  • Code in a shared repository
  • Built to the published standards
Ask for help early
A short conversation with a developer can redirect a hundred hours of work.
Renew every twelve months
Confirm it is still used and still needed, or it is disabled and loses data access.
Lane 2 is not lesser
It can be a polished, well-built app. What differs is who owns it.
LANE 2 → LANE 3Any one of these triggers it
The triggers
  • More than roughly 25 users
  • Touches PII, PHI, PCI, or financial data
  • Gains external or customer-facing access
  • Another system depends on its output
  • A process halts if it stops for a day
It starts with an evaluation
Not a rewrite. Pull the code, find the gaps against Lane 3 requirements, produce a plan.
Then a decision
Some gaps can be closed in place. Some need rebuilding. That call comes after looking, not before.
Lane 3 is still built with AI
The difference is the requirements it is built against, not the tools used.
Triggers are tests you apply to yourself. They are written to be objective so a builder can recognise the moment without waiting to be told. A working Lane 2 does not reduce Lane 3 demand — it increases it, because more apps become successful enough to matter.

→SUPPORT & GOVERNANCE — WHO PICKS UP WHAT

SUPPORT MODELBeing defined
Ownership and support are different
You own what you build, through Lane 1 and Lane 2. Registering does not hand it over. An app only changes hands when it changes lanes.
Lane 1 — nobody supports it
The trade for total freedom.
Lane 2 — IT supports it
Platform, hosting, identity, connections, and consulting on the logic. You still own the app.
Lane 3 — the dev team
Owns and supports it outright, with an SLA. Ownership transfers here — deliberately, with an evaluation first.
What we do not say
"We cannot look at it until it becomes a project and starts over." That answer pushes people off the road.
GOVERNANCE IN THE DATANot in the app
The principle
Permissions live with the data, not with each app. A badly built app still cannot reach what it should not.
How access actually works
No one gets into the database. An app asks a tool, and the tool decides what comes back — name, status, work location, and nothing else.
The controls
  • Entra SSO and role-scoped endpoints
  • Secrets vaulted, never in generated code
  • Per-app identity, so revocation is surgical
  • Audit logging on every connection
The default is open
Company data is available to you and your AI agents. The exceptions are sensitive HR data — pay rates, birthdays and the like — and Paylocity API access. You should not have to ask for anything outside that.
Why it matters
The worst case becomes a broken app rather than a data breach.
BEING WORKED OUTYour input shapes these
Who owns each data source
Knowing who to ask about payroll, warehouse, or finance data — so requests have somewhere to go and answers come back quickly.
Who decides on data access
The default is settled — open, except sensitive HR data and Paylocity. What is still open is who approves an exception and who that escalates to.
What moves an app up a lane
The triggers above are the current draft. We want them clear enough that you recognise the moment yourself.
How Lane 2 gets supported
Who helps when the owner is unavailable, and what that help looks like. Being defined with the teams involved.
Is 1.5 a lane or an on-ramp?
Shown here as an on-ramp within Lane 1. Still under discussion.
This is a draft, and that is deliberate. The lanes, the triggers, and the support model are still being settled — partly so the people who will actually build in them get a say first. If something here would get in your way, say so now.
