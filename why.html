Software Has Left the Dev Team

A short briefing on what is already happening, and what we are doing about it

The why

This is not something coming.
It already happened.

People across Redmond are building working software today — without the development team, without a budget line, and in many cases without telling anyone. AI removed the hard part of getting started. The next hundred apps will not be built by developers.

What we found

We thought there were ten or twenty. There are more than a hundred.

122apps, integrations, APIs and services in the registry today
13of those are truly mission critical — the rest are not
1development team, which did not build most of them

None of that is a problem by itself. Most of these are useful, and the people who built them solved a real problem faster than we could have. The problem is that we had no way of knowing what existed, what it touched, or who would fix it when it broke.

What it means

Two things are true at once

Freedom is the point. People should be able to try things without asking permission. That is how most of these got built, and we do not want to slow it down.

Some of it has to be dependable. When something breaks at two in the morning and the company runs on it, somebody has to be on the hook for it. That cannot be an individual and a side project.

So the question is not should people build. They already are. The question is how we tell the difference between the two — without putting a gate in front of everything.

The answer

Three lanes

Every app sits in one of three lanes. The lane is decided by what the app needs and touches — not by who built it, and not by how important it feels.

Lane 1Explore

Try anything. Personal tools, experiments, throwaway ideas. Nothing touches company data.

Nothing

Real tools other people use. Touches company data, so it goes on the register and uses the standard setup.

Lane 3Engineered

The systems the company runs on. Built, owned and supported to a standard, with someone accountable around the clock.

Register
Yes
Support
Development team, under an SLA

Development team

An app can move up a lane when it earns it. That is a deliberate step with a conversation attached, not a surprise — the team taking it on gets to look at it first and decide whether it comes across as is.

What this protects

Most of Redmond never notices any of this

  • No approval queue to try something. Lane 1 registers nothing and asks no one.
  • The rules land only where the risk actually is — company data, and systems we depend on.
  • People keep building. We stop being surprised by what they built.
  • When something matters enough to be supported properly, there is a path to that instead of a rebuild.

Who decides

The AI Governance Team

A standing group of seven, drawn from teams that already exist. It meets weekly. It is not a new department, and it does not operate anything.

What it decides, and who runs it day to day
Sets the directionRuns it day to day
What counts as sensitive dataData team
Access, identity and rolesIT team
The register and what stays on itDevelopment team
Approved tools and third-party appsApp team
Development standards and reviewDevelopment team

Why the split matters. If this group started operating things, every access request in the company would wait on a weekly meeting. It sets the standard. The team whose job it already is does the work.

Where we are

What we are asking for

  • Agreement that this is real and already here, not a forecast.
  • Agreement that the three lanes are the right shape — freedom by default, structure where it is earned.
  • Endorsement of the governance team as the group that sets direction, so the answers stop being made up one at a time.

The register is live. The lane model is documented. The governance team is meeting weekly. Development standards come after those two are settled — deliberately last.
