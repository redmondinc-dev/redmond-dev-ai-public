→THE MAGIC 8 — EIGHT THINGS EVERY PROJECT HAS TO ANSWER

What is a Magic 8? It is the project charter — a single page that answers eight questions before work starts. Not paperwork for its own sake: if you cannot answer all eight, the project is not ready, and that is worth knowing early. It stays a living document, revised as things change rather than written once and filed.

Four of the eight are about people. That is not an accident. Most projects do not fail on scope or cost — they fail because nobody knew who decided, who was affected, or who would keep it running afterwards. Support is the one most often left blank, and it is the one that determines whether anything you build survives its first year.

→THE ROLES — WHO DOES WHAT ON A PROJECT

Three things every project must do Collaborate to discover the best answer, build it, and support it. The Magic 8 is how you describe a project. The four roles are how you run one.

Why "Scout"? The name comes from Julia Galef's The Scout Mindset. A soldier's job is to defend a position; a scout's job is to go out, survey the territory, and come back with an accurate map — regardless of what they were hoping to find. Redmond picked the word on purpose. The person doing discovery is not there to defend a preferred answer.

Stakeholders sit at the center. Sponsor, Scout/Facilitator, and Support ring around them. The people affected by the work are the point of it — the other three roles exist to serve that center, not the other way round.

STAKEHOLDERSAt the center of all of it
The people affected by the work
Not a supporting role — the reason the project exists.

Look past your own team
The best answer often serves several teams at once.
If you know these by other names: the Scout does roughly what a business analyst does, and the Facilitator does roughly what a project manager does. Redmond deliberately uses Scout and Facilitator instead. "Manager" invites the failure of treating it as your project; "facilitator" describes the actual job. Same for Scout over analyst — the point is the mindset, not the deliverable.

Scout and Facilitator are a relay, not a handoff. The Scout owns the front half — understanding the problem and shaping the Why, Who and What. The Facilitator owns the back half — the What and How, and the running of it. They overlap deliberately around the point the sponsor approves, so nothing is dropped between them.

→WHERE IT GOES WRONG — HOW TO SPOT IT IN YOURSELF

Every role has a way of drifting These are the common failures, written down so they can be named early. Recognizing your own is the point — none of these is unusual, and all of them are recoverable if someone says so out loud.

Nearly all of them are the same failure: slipping from scout into soldier. Defending a position instead of surveying the ground. Jumping to a solution, getting boxed into one story, arriving with the answer already decided, being protective of your own problem — every one of those is defending territory rather than mapping it.

Two of these show up on nearly every project: rushing the process, and treating support as an afterthought. They are related. Rushing is what skips the support conversation, and skipping it is what leaves something running with nobody behind it a year later.
